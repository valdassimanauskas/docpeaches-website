Runner's Knee (Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome)

Understanding Runner's Knee: Causes, Symptoms, and Treatment Options

Despite its name, Runner's Knee does not only affect runners.

In fact, many patients diagnosed with Runner's Knee have never run a race in their lives.

Runner's Knee, also known as Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome (PFPS), is one of the most common causes of pain in the front of the knee and frequently affects:

  • Runners
  • Pickleball players
  • Tennis players
  • CrossFit athletes
  • Cyclists
  • Hikers
  • Active adults
  • Young athletes

Because the condition often develops gradually, many patients struggle to identify exactly when the problem started.

Understanding why Runner's Knee occurs is often the first step toward identifying appropriate treatment options.

What Is Runner's Knee?

Runner's Knee is a broad term commonly used to describe pain originating from the area around the kneecap (patella) and the structures that support it.

The condition involves the relationship between:

  • The kneecap
  • The thigh bone (femur)
  • Supporting muscles
  • Tendons
  • Soft tissues surrounding the knee

When movement patterns, loading forces, or joint mechanics become disrupted, pain may develop.

Why Is It Called Runner's Knee?

The condition earned its name because it frequently affects runners.

However, any activity involving repetitive knee bending may contribute to symptoms, including:

  • Squatting
  • Lunging
  • Stair climbing
  • Cycling
  • Jumping
  • Hiking
  • Sports participation

This is why many non-runners develop Runner's Knee as well.

Common Symptoms Of Runner's Knee

Patients may experience:

  • Pain in the front of the knee
  • Pain around the kneecap
  • Pain while climbing stairs
  • Pain during squats
  • Pain while running
  • Pain after prolonged sitting
  • Knee stiffness
  • Activity-related discomfort

Symptoms often worsen during activities that increase stress around the kneecap.

Why Does It Hurt When Going Down Stairs?

One of the classic complaints associated with Runner's Knee is:

"My knee hurts more going down stairs than going up."

Descending stairs increases the forces traveling through the kneecap and surrounding structures.

This often makes symptoms more noticeable.

Why Does My Knee Hurt After Sitting?

Many patients notice discomfort after:

  • Long car rides
  • Airplane travel
  • Watching movies
  • Sitting at a desk

This phenomenon is sometimes referred to as the "movie theater sign."

Remaining in a bent-knee position for extended periods may aggravate symptoms.

What Causes Runner's Knee?

Several factors may contribute, including:

Training Errors

Sudden increases in activity volume or intensity.

Muscle Imbalances

Changes in strength and movement control.

Biomechanical Factors

Movement patterns that increase stress on the knee.

Sports Participation

Repetitive loading from athletic activities.

Flexibility Limitations

Restricted movement within surrounding tissues.

In many cases, multiple factors contribute simultaneously.

Is Runner's Knee The Same As Arthritis?

No.

Runner's Knee and arthritis are different conditions.

Although both may cause knee pain, they involve different structures and mechanisms.

A comprehensive evaluation helps distinguish between potential causes.

How Is Runner's Knee Diagnosed?

A comprehensive evaluation typically includes:

  • Medical history
  • Physical examination
  • Functional assessment
  • Movement analysis
  • Review of symptoms

Diagnostic studies may include:

  • X-rays
  • MRI
  • Additional imaging when appropriate

The goal is to identify contributing factors and develop an individualized treatment strategy.

Treatment Considerations

Treatment recommendations depend on:

  • Symptom severity
  • Activity goals
  • Functional limitations
  • Movement patterns
  • Overall knee health

Potential treatment discussions may include:

  • Physical therapy
  • Strengthening programs
  • Activity modifications
  • Running modifications
  • Exercise progression strategies
  • Interventional procedures
  • Regenerative medicine discussions when appropriate

The best treatment strategy is individualized.

Can You Continue Exercising?

Many patients are able to remain active while managing Runner's Knee.

The appropriate approach depends on:

  • Symptom severity
  • Activity type
  • Functional limitations
  • Individual goals

The objective is often to help patients continue participating in meaningful activities while addressing contributing factors.

When Should You Seek Evaluation?

You may benefit from evaluation if:

  • Pain persists despite rest.
  • Running becomes difficult.
  • Stairs consistently aggravate symptoms.
  • Exercise participation is limited.
  • Symptoms interfere with daily activities.
  • Previous treatment has not provided sufficient improvement.

Understanding the source of symptoms is often the first step toward improvement.

The Dr. Peaches Perspective

Dr. Peaches believes that active people should not automatically be told to stop doing what they love.

The goal is not simply to reduce pain.

The goal is to understand why the pain developed and create a strategy that helps patients remain active whenever possible.

Whether the goal is completing a marathon, playing pickleball, hiking with family, or staying fit, every treatment plan should begin with understanding the individual patient.

The mission remains simple:

Help people stay in the game.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Runner's Knee?

Runner's Knee, or Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome, is a common condition involving pain around the kneecap.

Do you have to be a runner to get Runner's Knee?

No.

Many active adults and athletes develop the condition without participating in running.

Why does my knee hurt going down stairs?

Stair descent increases forces around the kneecap and may aggravate symptoms.

Is Runner's Knee the same as arthritis?

No.

They are different conditions involving different structures within the knee.

Can I continue exercising?

Many patients remain active, although exercise recommendations should be individualized.

Should I bring prior imaging to my consultation?

Yes.

Imaging studies and previous treatment records can provide valuable information during the evaluation process.

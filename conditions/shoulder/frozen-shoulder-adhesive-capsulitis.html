Frozen Shoulder (Adhesive Capsulitis)

Understanding Frozen Shoulder: Causes, Symptoms, and Treatment Options

Many patients describe frozen shoulder the same way:

"I didn't just have shoulder pain. I woke up one day and realized I couldn't move my arm the way I used to."

Unlike many shoulder injuries that occur after a specific event, frozen shoulder often develops gradually. Patients may first notice mild discomfort, followed by increasing stiffness and a progressive loss of motion.

Simple activities such as:

  • Reaching overhead
  • Fastening a bra
  • Reaching into the back seat of a car
  • Putting on a jacket
  • Washing your hair

can become surprisingly difficult.

Understanding frozen shoulder is often the first step toward understanding why shoulder movement becomes restricted and what treatment options may be available.

What Is Frozen Shoulder?

Frozen shoulder, also known as Adhesive Capsulitis, is a condition involving pain and progressive loss of shoulder motion.

The condition affects the capsule surrounding the shoulder joint.

As the condition develops, patients often experience:

  • Pain
  • Stiffness
  • Reduced mobility
  • Difficulty performing everyday activities

One of the defining characteristics of frozen shoulder is that both pain and motion become affected.

What Makes Frozen Shoulder Different?

Many shoulder conditions cause pain.

Frozen shoulder causes:

Pain

AND

Loss Of Motion

This distinction is important.

Patients with rotator cuff injuries may have pain but often retain a significant amount of shoulder motion.

Patients with frozen shoulder frequently notice that the shoulder simply does not move normally.

Common Symptoms Of Frozen Shoulder

Patients may experience:

  • Shoulder pain
  • Progressive stiffness
  • Difficulty reaching overhead
  • Difficulty reaching behind the back
  • Difficulty dressing
  • Pain at night
  • Sleep disturbances
  • Loss of shoulder mobility

Symptoms often worsen gradually over time.

Why Does Frozen Shoulder Develop?

The exact cause is not always clear.

Several factors have been associated with frozen shoulder, including:

  • Previous shoulder injuries
  • Periods of shoulder immobilization
  • Certain medical conditions
  • Previous surgery
  • Inflammation involving the shoulder capsule

Many patients cannot identify a single event that caused their symptoms.

Who Is Most Likely To Develop Frozen Shoulder?

Frozen shoulder can affect a wide range of individuals but is often seen in:

  • Adults between 40 and 65 years old
  • Individuals recovering from shoulder injuries
  • Individuals recovering from surgery
  • Patients with certain metabolic conditions

Every patient's situation is unique.

The Three Phases Of Frozen Shoulder

Patients often hear physicians describe frozen shoulder in phases.

Freezing Phase

Pain gradually develops and shoulder motion becomes increasingly limited.

Frozen Phase

Stiffness becomes more noticeable and shoulder mobility is significantly restricted.

Thawing Phase

Motion may gradually improve over time.

The duration of each phase varies significantly between individuals.

Why Is It Difficult To Reach Behind My Back?

One of the classic signs of frozen shoulder is difficulty performing movements such as:

  • Reaching into a back pocket
  • Fastening clothing
  • Tucking in a shirt
  • Reaching behind the body

These movements often become restricted early in the condition.

How Is Frozen Shoulder Diagnosed?

A comprehensive evaluation typically includes:

  • Medical history
  • Physical examination
  • Motion assessment
  • Functional evaluation
  • Review of symptoms

Diagnostic studies may include:

  • X-rays
  • MRI
  • Ultrasound
  • Additional imaging when appropriate

The goal is to distinguish frozen shoulder from other causes of shoulder pain and stiffness.

Frozen Shoulder vs Rotator Cuff Tear

Patients often confuse these conditions because both may cause shoulder pain.

However:

Rotator Cuff Tear

Typically involves tendon pathology.

Frozen Shoulder

Primarily involves pain and significant loss of motion.

The treatment considerations may differ, making accurate diagnosis important.

Treatment Considerations

Treatment recommendations depend on:

  • Symptom severity
  • Stage of the condition
  • Functional limitations
  • Activity goals

Potential treatment discussions may include:

  • Physical therapy
  • Mobility programs
  • Activity modifications
  • Home exercise strategies
  • Interventional procedures
  • Regenerative medicine discussions when appropriate

The best treatment strategy is individualized.

Can Frozen Shoulder Go Away On Its Own?

Frozen shoulder may improve over time in many individuals.

However, the timeline varies significantly.

Some patients experience symptoms for extended periods before noticing meaningful improvement.

This is why proper evaluation and guidance are important.

When Should You Seek Evaluation?

You may benefit from evaluation if:

  • Shoulder motion is becoming increasingly restricted.
  • Pain interferes with sleep.
  • Daily activities are becoming difficult.
  • Reaching overhead is challenging.
  • Reaching behind your back is difficult.
  • Symptoms continue to worsen.

Understanding the diagnosis is often the first step toward determining your options.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is frozen shoulder?

Frozen shoulder, or adhesive capsulitis, is a condition involving shoulder pain and progressive loss of motion.

Why can't I lift my arm normally?

Loss of shoulder mobility is one of the hallmark features of frozen shoulder.

Is frozen shoulder the same as a rotator cuff tear?

No.

Although both may cause shoulder pain, they involve different structures and different mechanisms.

Does frozen shoulder always require surgery?

No.

Many patients explore non-surgical treatment options depending on their symptoms and functional limitations.

How long does frozen shoulder last?

The duration varies significantly between individuals.

Should I bring prior imaging to my consultation?

Yes.

Imaging studies and treatment records provide valuable information during the evaluation process.
